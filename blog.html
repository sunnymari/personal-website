🎓 Education & AI

I'm deeply interested in how education, accessibility, and AI intersect — especially how we can use technology to build more supportive learning environments.

A short screen recording from my Education & AI work-in-progress.

Education Needs AI , and Not for the Reasons You Think I have been in the classroom as a tutor, aide, and teacher over the past decade. I have worked with some of the most underprivileged students and some of the most privileged. Growing up I wanted to be a fourth grade science teacher, but pivoted to software engineering which is my full-time career currently, but education never stopped being a passion of mine. The Interview That Changed My Perspective Recently I had an opportunity to interview at a school for a data analyst position and what I saw in the interview process opened my eyes to the failures of education but also the room to excel with AI. During the interview process I was asked to grade 50 papers and model the data. I knew this was a tedious process so naturally I had to get creative. I used Cursor Code to create an RAG system based upon the worksheet concepts and an LLM to grade the assignments and to display the data I made an interactive dashboard. This dashboard displayed student data, did performance analysis and also had an AI chatbot which could be used to ask about the students data. I did this all within an hour. Shifting How We View Student Data The response from the school was overwhelmingly positive. The dashboard bridged the insight gap. The student data was no longer reactive but it shifted to proactive and personalized. Teachers could see what concepts each individual student needed improvement with actionable insights on things the student could do to improve. It also modeled the data of all the students together identifying content gaps for certain concepts. With this tool teachers would no longer have to be reactive with student data in real-time teachers can form actionable plans to combat low test scores and concept gaps. The Big Picture Vision But it's not just for education data, it can be used to unify all classroom data. Student Information Systems , Learning Management Systems, assessments, and behavior data such as attendance/ classroom conduct can be viewed all in one platform by layering a custom LLM with retrieval-augmented generation trained on district policies, curriculum, and approved interventions. Rather than static dashboards, educators get: Early risk alerts Small-group rosters Lesson-level recommendations Ready-to-use artifacts like lesson openers and parent messages All delivered through a teacher copilot that explains the why in plain language and cites its sources. On average, this could save teachers 1–2 hours per week, help them act earlier, teach smarter, and better support every learner , especially those who are most underserved. The Outcome While the response from the school was positive, there was not enough room in the budget to hire me.

You Can't Hurt Me

August 12, 2026

Can't Hurt Me by David Goggins is one of the most relatable books I've ever read.

I found it while googling books to read when you're going through a tough time. At that point I was still in the middle of an ongoing health battle, with a second appointment with my oncologist and hematologist still looming. I had already left a software engineering job I loved so I could move closer to my family (begrudgingly, they're complicated), help them financially, and get care from doctors who'd actually known me for more than one visit.

Reading it felt less like discovering a new philosophy and more like someone finally putting language to how I'd already been living for 25 years: go through it, keep going, don't whine about it. Goggins calls this "callusing the mind," the idea that the more you willingly walk into hard things and come out the other side, the less any single hard thing can break you. I didn't read that concept and learn it. I read it and recognized it.

So here's the s**t I went through. Hell and back. I got my first tech job at 17. Not through a career fair or a referral. I cold-called startups in my area until one said yes. That instinct, figure it out yourself, ask before you're ready, started a lot earlier than that.

My first memory of domestic violence was when I was four. I knew I wanted out of my home so in eighth grade I applied and successfully got into Madeira, one of the most exclusive boarding schools in the country, with financial aid. I wasn't allowed to go. I stayed in the situation I was trying to leave, 25+ years of domestic violence in my home, and rebuilt my plan from public school instead. I tested into Maryland's top gifted and talented program on a perfect literature/quant PSAT score (same program Sergey Brin came out of, for what it's worth). I was one of the only kids there who didn't fit the room, and I got bullied for it by people on both sides of that line. I switched high schools three times in four years, which happened to be the peak of the abuse at home. CPS spoke to me three separate times. A foster placement almost happened and then didn't.

I still won the National Center for Women in Technology's Aspirations in Computing Award twice. Did FIRST Robotics. Landed a tech internship at 17. Stayed on varsity swim the whole time. My GPA, a 4.3 sophomore and junior year, did dip under the weight of everything else, though I still walked away with college credit from those AP classes so in short a win is a win. I went to an HBCU instead of the Ivy League I'd always pictured: full ride, big fish in a small pond, still commuting from the same house, still interning at a Fortune 500 company on the side. Independence of thought, to me, has never been an aesthetic. It's what happens when nobody's going to build the ladder for you, so you build your own and you climb it anyway.

Some highlights from that stretch:

  • Accepted into Management Leadership for Tomorrow as a Career Prep Fellow
  • HBCU Venture Capital Fellow
  • Founded a Girls Who Code club at my university that's still active today
  • AmeriCorps, where I earned a perfect teaching score and now sit on the alumni advisory board representing California
  • Ran my own tutoring company and worked in schools as a tutor
  • Transferred to a PWI from my HBCU and took 46 credits between January and August 2024, pulling a 4.0 that summer semester while testing out of a large chunk of the requirements, for a computer science degree, not exactly the easy way to do it

I did all of this while dealing with hell at home: domestic violence, emotional abuse, financial abuse, and acting like everything was fine in public. If you know, you know.

During that same period, I lost four people: my two aunts on my mother's side, my godmother, and my cousin, who was shot before he ever got the chance to walk his graduation stage.

His death landed right in the middle of the most brutal stretch of that 46-credit semester. It should have broken my focus completely. Instead it did the opposite. It made staying exactly where I was, doing exactly what I'd been doing, feel impossible. There was nothing left for me back where I was from, and I knew it with total clarity.

Goggins talks about a "cookie jar," a mental reserve of every hard thing you've already survived, that you reach into when the current hard thing wants to convince you that you can't. I didn't have a name for it back then, but I was already doing it. Every time I wanted to stop, I pulled from everything I'd already outlasted and kept moving. Like Mario running through the level: you don't stop just because you got hit. You keep it moving.

Still Showing Up

I graduated and moved to San Diego to work at Homes.com, where I survived two rounds of layoffs and pulled stellar performance reviews the whole way through, the kind of thing that reads like a clean bullet point on LinkedIn and was actually just relentlessness wearing a different outfit.

The entire time I was in San Diego, I was also freelancing and tutoring on the side. Not for extra savings. Because I was supporting three family members back on the East Coast who were dealing with health issues of their own. I kept making my deadlines. I kept showing up. There was a day I passed out at daily standup and got sick at my desk, and I still found a way to push through and keep moving. Eventually the math stopped working: San Diego rent plus money sent home wore me down faster than I could keep up with, on top of stress-related health issues of my own that needed real care. So I moved back to help my family more directly and finally get seen by doctors I trusted.

I don't tell that part to make it sound noble. It wasn't sustainable, and I know that now. But it's true, and it's part of how I got here.

Around that time, I got into the Roblox Jumpstart program, where, thanks to the TikTok algorithm, I found my cofounder, Jasmine Chimona.

My abuser insisted on accompanying me to the Roblox Inspire Cafe event in NYC. The night after the conference, they assaulted me.

I kept going. The police were called. They had taken my cards and cash. With the help of members of my chosen family, I got a new hotel room, and the next morning I still showed up for our promotional photoshoot for our Roblox game with my cofounder. I didn't tell her what had happened. I just did the work, because I wanted everyone on the team to still get to have a good day. I don't say that to be a martyr about it. I say it because it's the most honest example I have of what leadership has actually cost me, and what I chose to protect anyway.

Another incident followed, and I left NYC with a member of my chosen family, traveled up the coast, and got a restraining order against my abuser. I wore men's clothes and random hand-me-downs, because all I had with me were the clothes from the conference. Then I moved to the SF Bay Area to finish my master's.

What Nobody Tells You About Waiting

In court, I was asked why I hadn't left sooner. Why, once CPS failed me and I'd accepted the situation as my norm, I didn't cut ties even while living across the country from my abuser. In the meantime, wellness checks kept getting sent to my apartment, a form of retaliation, and I didn't want it to escalate into police showing up at my job.

So I waited.

Domestic abuse is a trap either way you plan it. Move too fast without enough in place, and you end up back in the same situation, except worse. Move too slow trying to get everything perfectly lined up, and you stay stagnant while the cycle just continues. It's a waiting game. I waited, and in the waiting, I built a career and finished my education anyway.

Eventually, I found my opening: the assault. It gave me my restraining order and let me finally cut ties for good.

Domestic violence is tricky, and it doesn't always come with visible signs. I was forced to look put-together to hide what I was living through, or threatened with more violence if I tried to report it. If you're dealing with something similar, I'm sending you love and support.

Getting assaulted became the catalyst, the moment where everything I'd been carrying finally surfaced somewhere it couldn't be hidden anymore. In a strange way, it was one of the best things that ever happened to me.

Moving Forward

Now I'm actively enrolled in therapy, and actually planning to enjoy the rest of my twenties.

Goggins has this idea he calls the accountability mirror: sitting with yourself and telling the truth about what actually happened, instead of the version that's easier to live with. That's what writing this has been. Not a highlight reel. The truth, at full volume, for once.

So his title turned out to be right. You can't hurt me. I'll just get back up.

🚀 Four Things I Have Learned from Working at a Startup

📅 August 18, 2025 | Previously published on my Medium in 2022

I am a rising junior in college and a computer science major with a passion for science — especially computer science. So when I was able to apply what I had been learning at school as an engineer for a startup (not just any startup, but one that rebuilds the internet for you), I said YES. Throughout my time working as a software engineer at Sonr, I have learned things I would not have in the traditional college setting. Here are the four main lessons I have learned:

1. School is a starting point — it is up to you to finish the rest

My computer science coursework mainly focused on object-oriented programming, and the fact that I have taken applicable math and science courses has aided in the comprehension of my work tasks. However, working in the field has gifted me with irreplaceable knowledge. For example, in the classroom, we are not working in teams, so using Git Flow did not apply to the scope of our coursework. While working at Sonr, I have been able to learn and apply Git Flow. Git Flow is one of the most common tools used by engineering companies; it describes the project's release cycle and adds specific roles to different branches.

2. You don't learn to swim by reading a book on how to swim

During the school year, one of my greatest resources was my textbook; it was essential to read before lectures. However, to be a good programmer, you don't just read or solve school problems; you build. I had two goals this year: get a certification and add more to my GitHub because my GitHub was actually barren. I can proudly say I have achieved both goals. I received my Chainalysis Cryptocurrency certification, and my GitHub has new additions from work and personal projects. I used to fear personal projects, but with the coaching of my coworkers, I have begun to make them. Currently, I am working on personal projects that intertwine my love of art and science, such as a Y2K-inspired GitHub-hosted website and a pink tic-tac-toe game.

3. Feel stupid every day

Before my job, I had reached a plateau. I was doing well in school, but I knew there were things I needed to learn because "the wise man knows what he does not know." Working at a startup gave me the ability to learn what I didn't know and learn things ahead of when I would be in school. Therefore, I am confident this upcoming fall semester will be less taxing than previous semesters.

4. Read about everything

In our office, there are a lot of books. Books about math, science, philosophy, and more — you name it. One thing I've noticed from reading and absorbing a multitude of literature is that it makes you a better thinker and person. By exposing yourself to a variety of literature, you open your mind to diverse thoughts and perspectives and start to see the world differently.

Working at Sonr has made me a better learner, thinker, developer, and person. It has shown me how to be resilient, work harder, and adapt to change. This is your sign to go work at a startup. ✨

👔 The Manager: Meeting Your Workplace Fate (and How to Prepare)

📅 August 15, 2025

The manager, also known as the person who decides your workplace fate (just kidding, but only a little). Good managers are critical in one's first job out of undergrad. They play a pivotal role in the trajectory of one's career growth and professional development.

I met my manager on Superday when he interviewed me. Ultimately, my Superday was successful, and I got hired for the job—yay! However, being hired is one thing; doing well on the job is another. I believe in the power of preparation because, as Benjamin Franklin said, "By failing to prepare, you are preparing to fail."

Therefore, I decided to meet with my manager months before my start date to ensure I had knowledge of the tech stack and more insight into the company culture. I prepared for the meeting by making a list of questions reviewed by my career coach. Each question was divided into three categories: tech stack, company culture, and engineering team culture.

📋 My Question List:

  1. What are the expectations within the first 30 days of the job?
  2. What is your culture around problem-solving?
  3. When will I have a chance to meet the team?
  4. What project will I be joining what are the expectations - goals -and outcomes?
  5. What do you like about the culture, what would you change?
  6. How can I be an asset to the culture?
  7. Will we be meeting regular basis?
  8. Who will I be asking questions in the beginning?
  9. What does Day 1 look like/ what are the logistics?
  10. What technical things I can learn now to prepare?

I highly encourage every new SWE to set up a meeting with their manager early to begin building a strong relationship. ✨
